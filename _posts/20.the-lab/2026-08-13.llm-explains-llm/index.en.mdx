<TLDR>
  An LLM is a stateless text-continuation function, trained into a lossy
  generative index of human text, wrapped in a loop of ordinary code that gives
  it memory (the context window), hands (tools), and a steering wheel
  (everything you put in the window).
</TLDR>

That sentence is a claim about a mechanism, and a claim about a mechanism is only earned one way: by exhibiting the mechanism. So we will do what an engineer does with any black box — push one concrete input through it and watch what happens to it at every stage.

Our input, for the whole page, is one phrase:

> **The Eiffel Tower is in**

Not a question with an answer — a sentence with a gap at the end, the exact shape a language model exists to fill. Every stage below asks the same thing: given this phrase, what does the model do with it, one step at a time?

## Act 1 — Inference: one phrase, traced

### 1.1 Embedding

Inside the model there is nothing but arithmetic — additions and multiplications over arrays of numbers. Letters are not numbers, so the model cannot consume our phrase as text; something must convert it first. That converter is not part of the learned model at all: it is built separately, before training, by counting which character sequences co-occur in real text — no gradient ever touches it — and it ships as ordinary code. So it belongs to the harness, and its name is the **tokenizer**.

The essence of a tokenizer fits in one sentence: **it holds a fixed list of text pieces — the vocabulary, 50,257 entries for the model on this page — and cuts your text into pieces from that list, outputting each piece's index number.** Everything else is consequence. A frequent word earned its own entry in the list, so it survives as one piece. A rare word never earned an entry, so it gets cut into fragments of entries that did. Text from outside the list's usual world — another alphabet, say — falls all the way back to raw bytes.

Watch all three outcomes below. Byte-pair encoding builds each cut by starting from raw bytes and repeatedly merging whichever neighboring pair the vocabulary ranks highest; step through a preset's rounds and watch the merges happen: in each round, every word merges its highest-ranked neighboring pair. Our phrase survives mostly as one piece per word — "The", " is", " in" each keep their whole selves — except the rare name, which shatters: " E" + "iff" + "el". Ask the same algorithm about bare "Tower" and it stalls one merge short of a single piece, "T" + "ower" (ids 51 and 789); glue the leading space back on and " Tower" merges all the way down to one piece, id 8765, because "the word with a space in front" and "the word alone" are different entries in the vocabulary. Ask it about the Ukrainian version of the phrase, "Ейфелева вежа знаходиться в", and almost nothing merges: 32 pieces come out, mostly raw UTF-8 bytes shown as hex, because the vocabulary's merges were learned from a mostly-English, mostly-Latin-alphabet corpus.

<TokenizerDemo />

Two details in the output matter later. First, the `␣` glyph marks a piece that starts with a space — exactly the split you just watched, bare "Tower" against " Tower": a space usually isn't a token of its own, it rides glued onto the piece after it. Second, look at the ids under the chips, because they carry a warning: the ids of related pieces are unrelated. "iff" is 733; " Tower" is 8765 — two pieces of the same phrase, two numbers that say nothing about that fact. A token id works like a **warehouse SKU**: it says _which_ entry of the list this is, and nothing else — adjacent numbers are not related items. It carries no meaning.

So our phrase is now seven integers — `464 412 733 417 8765 318 287` — and integers that carry no meaning are useless on their own. **Meaning is exactly what the next stage manufactures.**

The model's first layer is a lookup table. **Indexing that table with a token id returns the token's embedding: a learned vector of 768 numbers, one row of a 50,257×768 table — about 39M parameters just for this lookup.** A lookup breaks no rules of the arithmetic-only machine, because indexing a table _is_ arithmetic: multiply the table by a one-hot vector and the id's row falls out. This is the moment text enters the model's native medium — an id that was only a label becomes an array of numbers that arithmetic can act on.

But swapping a label for an array only helps if the numbers _mean_ something, and they do — not by design, but by training (Act 2 shows the force; here is its result). The figure below shows the tokenizer's output feeding the lookup, then the two properties that make the looked-up numbers meaningful:

<EmbeddingFigure />

The figure's second step: each id indexes its row of the 768-wide table, drawn as a strip of colour intensity (8 of the 768 dimensions shown). Flatten those 768 dimensions onto a page and you see the first property: **nearby means related.** "Eiffel Tower" and "Colosseum" sit together as landmarks; "Paris", "Rome" and "France" sit together as their cities, some distance away. The second property is stronger than proximity: **directions mean relations.** The arrow from "Eiffel Tower" to "Paris" points the same way as the arrow from "Colosseum" to "Rome", because "landmark → its city" became a consistent displacement in the space. Everything drawn is hand-placed to illustrate (real learned geometry is only statistically this tidy) — the two properties are what is real.

Why does this matter so much? Because the model is an arithmetic-only machine — that was the very first fact of this section — and arithmetic cannot process "meaning" unless meaning has been turned into geometry. Once related things are near and relations are directions, adding and multiplying vectors _is_ operating on meaning.

One thing is still missing, and it's the figure's third step. Nothing said so far tells the model _where_ each token sits: two ids would embed identically whichever position they occupied, so "The Eiffel Tower is in" and a shuffled "is The in Eiffel Tower" would produce the same seven vectors, just reordered. GPT-2's fix is a second lookup, this one indexed by position instead of by token id: a learned 1,024×768 table (1,024 is the longest sequence the model was trained to place), one row per slot in the sequence. (Current models more often use a cheaper trick, RoPE: rotate the query and key vectors as a function of distance instead of storing a table at all — one more piece of learned arithmetic, not new machinery.)

The figure's fourth step, final embedding, is just the sum: token embedding plus positional encoding, one 768-number vector per position, shape (7, 768) for our seven tokens. Our phrase is now seven points in that space, and the single operation the model uses to work on those points is next.

### 1.2 Multi-head self-attention

Almost everything the model computes from here on is one operation: multiply a vector by a matrix of learned weights. This section teaches that operation on its first real job — building queries, keys and values for attention — and the MLP, next, and the final word choice, several sections on, are both built from the same operation. Earn it once, properly, and nothing later is hand-waving.

Every position in our phrase computes three vectors from its embedding: a **query** — "what am I looking for?"; a **key** — "what am I about?"; a **value** — "what do I hand over if you look at me?" All three come from one matmul, run once: multiply the (7, 768) matrix of our seven embeddings by one learned (768, 2304) weight matrix, add a bias, and the (7, 2304) result splits into three 768-wide bands side by side, Q, K and V.

<MatmulFigure variant='plain' />

Read it as the figure draws it. Each column of that (768, 2304) weight matrix is a detector, the same 768-number pattern held up against every one of the seven token vectors in turn. Hover an output cell and its column lights up: a dot product, large and positive when the token's values agree with the column's pattern, near zero when unrelated, negative when they oppose. The output is the list of detector responses — how strongly each of the 2,304 patterns is present in that token. The right metaphor is a **bank of stencils**: one matmul holds every stencil up against the same input at once and records how well each fits. This figure holds up as many stencils as it draws columns; a real layer holds up thousands, and that is the entire difference. Below the projection, the figure shows what a nonlinearity would do to one of these rows: **GELU** squashes small or negative values toward zero and lets large positive ones through almost unchanged. Q, K and V themselves carry no nonlinearity; GELU is shown here because it's the one this network actually uses, in the MLP, next.

One honest repair before moving on. Interpretability research shows that a single real column rarely detects a single clean human concept — features come out smeared across many columns at once ("superposition" → [Anthropic, "Toy Models of Superposition"](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html)). The detector picture is still the right way to _read_ a matmul; it is just the wrong anatomy of _individual columns_. Detectors exist; they simply don't sit one-per-column.

Q, K and V then split again — not the model doing anything new, just how the 768 numbers get read. GPT-2 small treats each as 12 heads of 64 numbers, 12 independent attention lookups running in parallel, each with its own slice of Q, K and V. One head can learn to track who-refers-to-whom, another syntax, another simply copies patterns it has already seen earlier in the same document — that last kind returns with a starring role in 3.2.

Whether position A looks at position B is decided by how well A's query matches B's key, and "how well does this match" is exactly what a dot product measures — the same operation as above, just Q against K this time. The figure below draws it for one head, as three 7×7 grids over our seven tokens:

<MatmulFigure variant='attention' />

The first grid is the raw dot product, query row against key column, one score per pair of positions. The second scales every score down (dividing by √64, so the numbers don't saturate what comes next) and applies the **causal mask**: every cell above the diagonal — a position trying to look at a _later_ position — is blanked before use. The reason is the model's job description: it must predict the next token, and a prediction that peeks at what comes after it is not a prediction. The third grid is **softmax**, turning each row into weights that sum to 1, big score gaps becoming decisive weights and small gaps staying soft. Each position then takes the weighted average of the _values_ of the positions it attends to: that average is the payload, information from earlier tokens arriving into this one's representation.

Twelve heads run this in parallel and hand back twelve 64-number results per position. Concatenate them into one 768-number vector, pass that through one more learned matrix — the output projection — and the result is ready to rejoin the stream. That is the whole of attention: three matmuls to build Q, K, V, one matmul-shaped lookup to decide who looks at whom, one more matmul to fold twelve heads' answers back into a single vector.

Toy shapes can't show you _why_ this is powerful, so watch it work on our actual phrase, across three illustrative heads. Hover 'el' under the **name builder** head: it reaches back to '␣E' and 'iff' and reassembles the three pieces into "Eiffel" — because 1.1's tokenizer had fractured the name into three pieces, and something has to put it back together before the model can reason about it. Hover any token under the **previous token** head: it mostly just watches the one right before it, simple positional bookkeeping. Hover '␣in' under **what are we locating**: it looks back hardest at '␣Tower' — file that; this is the head that carries forward the location the next section's MLP recalls there, all the way to the last position.

<AttentionDemo />

Here your favorite metaphor gets its precise scope. Attention is very nearly a search engine — query, keys, match scores, a result assembled from the matches — with one honest liberty: it never picks a winner, it blends _all_ visible matches by weight. Whether the whole _model_ is a search engine is a different question, and the epilogue settles it with a no. Keep the metaphor; keep it at this level.

Attention moves information between positions. It has no way to transform what a position already knows, on its own — that's the other machine's job, and it's next.

### 1.3 MLP

The **MLP** (multi-layer perceptron, the historical name for exactly what follows) is attention's opposite number: attention moves information between positions; the MLP transforms one position's information in place, running on each of the seven positions independently, the same weights applied to each in turn.

It's two more matmuls around one nonlinearity, at a much bigger scale than the Q/K/V projection you just watched. The figure below follows just the token '␣Tower', because that's the position where something interesting fires:

<MLPFigure />

768 numbers in, an expand matrix maps them up to 3,072 — four times wider: 3,072 detectors, each a stencil held up against the same 768-number input, exactly the mechanism from 1.2, just with far more columns. GELU comes next, the same squashing previewed in 1.2's figure: small or negative responses get pushed toward zero, large positive ones pass through almost unchanged. Here it isn't decorative — without it, expand-then-contract is two matrix multiplies back to back, and two matrix multiplies in a row collapse algebraically into one matrix multiply no matter how wide the middle is. The nonlinearity is what makes the width buy anything.

A contract matrix maps the surviving 3,072 numbers back down to 768, and this is where the writing happens: each detector that fired hands its own learned 768-number vector to the sum, and only the detectors GELU let through contribute. For '␣Tower', a handful of the 3,072 detectors are tuned to something like "Eiffel Tower", and when they fire, the vectors they write nudge the position's representation toward a new direction — the one that means "Paris". That result is added into the residual stream at '␣Tower', on top of the token and position information already sitting there from 1.1, not overwriting any of it.

This is the model's factual memory, and the mechanism has a name in the literature: a **key-value memory** — the expand matrix's columns are the keys (what pattern to notice), the contract matrix's rows are the values (what to write when noticed) → [Geva et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14913); [Meng et al., 2022, "ROME"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05262) locate exactly this kind of edit, at exactly this kind of position, in exactly this kind of model. Recall happens at the last token of the subject, '␣Tower', not at the position asking the question, '␣in' — which is exactly why 1.2's "what are we locating" head exists: it reads what the MLP wrote at '␣Tower' and carries it forward to the last position, where the output layer will read it off.

One MLP, one block: about 4.7M weights (two 768×3,072 matrices, plus biases), roughly two-thirds of everything a single transformer block learns. Multiply by 12 blocks, and most of what the model "knows" lives here, not in attention.

### 1.4 The block, the stream, the stack

The package has a name: a **transformer block** = one attention step (1.2) + one MLP (1.3). Communicate, then compute. The figure below shows one block, and the wiring detail that makes deep stacks of them possible:

<TransformerBlockDiagram />

Follow the thick vertical line. Each token's representation flows down a **residual stream**, and the block's two parts don't replace what flows past — each reads from it, behind its own LayerNorm (a rescaling step that keeps the numbers in a stable range), and _adds_ its contribution back in, the `+` junctions in the figure. Why adding matters: because nothing is overwritten, a block deep in the stack still sees the original embedding plus every refinement made so far, so each block only ever needs to learn a _correction_, not a full re-description. The right image is a **conveyor belt** through a line of work stations: every station inspects the item and attaches something; none rebuilds it from scratch.

GPT-2 small stacks this block 12 times (frontier models stack far more, and wider, blocks). The depth is not redundancy, it is composition: each block's detectors run on the _fingerprints_ produced by the block before — both attention's moved information and the MLP's written directions — so features compound, edges of meaning at the bottom, structures of structures further up. Interpretability work that peeks inside consistently finds a division of labor: early blocks resolving grammar and stitching fragments like " E" + "iff" + "el" back into one name, middle blocks assembling facts and relations (1.3's Eiffel-Tower-to-Paris recall is exactly this kind of middle work), late blocks converting "what is being said" into "what comes next". Observed regularity, not designed layout, but observed again and again.

That raises a cost problem worth flagging for later: every parameter in every block does arithmetic on every token, so making a model _know more_ makes every token _cost more_. Section 1.8 shows how modern models unweld that link.

All seven of our tokens ride the belt through every block, and only one of them still matters for what happens next. The causal mask (1.2) means the last position, '␣in', is the only one that has seen the entire phrase — carrying, thanks to 1.2's locating head, the Paris direction 1.3's MLP recalled two positions earlier. **So the last position's vector holds the model's whole opinion about what comes next, and that opinion must now be turned back into a word.**

### 1.5 Output probabilities

One final sequence of steps turns that vector back into words. First a LayerNorm rescales it, the same normalizing step used before every attention and MLP inside the stack. Then one more matmul — tied to the very lookup table from 1.1, the same 50,257×768 matrix, used here as (768, 50,257) instead of the other way around — scores that vector against all 50,257 vocabulary entries. The result is one raw score per token in the vocabulary, called a **logit**, and there the model _stops_. It does not choose a word; it hands back 50,257 scores, the same scores every time for the same input, because a pure function has no dice in it.

Everything after the logits belongs to the harness: the **sampler**. It runs the scores through softmax, the same recipe from 1.2's attention, to turn them into a probability distribution — this token some percent, that one some percent, a long tail of slivers — and then draws from that distribution. Try it below on our phrase's actual candidates:

<TemperatureDemo />

**Temperature** T rescales the logits before that softmax: drag it to 0.05 and the distribution collapses onto the single top token, greedy decoding, deterministic; drag it high and the distribution flattens toward adventurous, diverse draws. **Top-k** keeps only the k highest-scoring tokens and zeroes the rest before renormalizing (k=1 is greedy decoding by another route). **Top-p** does the same job by a different cut: keep the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to p, and zero everything outside it. All three knobs act after the logits are already fixed. A "sample one token" button actually draws once and appends the result below, though the table keeps scoring the original seven-token prompt; a real loop re-runs the whole forward pass on the longer prompt after every draw, which is exactly 1.7's subject. What you are proving to yourself is the section's whole point: the bars' _ordering_ never changes, because the knowledge is in the model, but the _choice policy_ is yours, because the dice are in the harness.

Suppose the sampler picks the front-runner: ' Paris', well ahead of the next candidate, ' France'. (At high temperature it may legitimately pick something else — that is not an error, that is the policy you chose.) Before the loop that turns this one token into a full continuation, three small pieces of machinery you've already met deserve to be named properly.

### 1.6 Auxiliary features

Three pieces of plumbing appeared already, without names. **LayerNorm** rescales a vector so its numbers sit in a stable range before each attention or MLP step (1.4's diagram shows exactly where); without it, the numbers passing down 12 stacked blocks would drift, and the training signal (Act 2) would struggle to reach the early blocks at all. **Dropout** randomly zeroes a fraction of activations during training only, forcing the network to spread what it learns across many detectors instead of leaning on a few; at inference — everything on this page — it's switched off, so it never touches the phrase you feed the model. The **residual stream** from 1.4 is the third: the addition, not the transformation, that lets 12 blocks stack without each one having to redo the last one's work.

None of the three teaches the model anything new; all three are what make the two real workhorses, attention and the MLP, trainable and stackable at all.

### 1.7 The loop

The harness code that turns a one-token function into a paragraph generator fits on a napkin:

```js
context = tokenize(prompt)
loop:
  dist  = f(context)            // the model: one forward pass, stateless
  token = sample(dist, T)       // the harness: your sampling policy
  context.append(token)
  if token == STOP: break
```

The figure below animates one lap of that loop — context in ①, forward pass ②, distribution ③, and the sampled token appended ④, becoming part of the next lap's input. Watch the append arrow: that is the only feedback path in the entire system. At the bottom, outside the animation, sits the destination: our completed answer after four laps, " Paris, France." — and one lap later the model would sample a stop token, the loop's exit condition.

<AutoregressiveLoop />

Say the load-bearing fact by itself: **the model is stateless.** No memory survives between calls; each lap, `f` receives the entire context from scratch, one token longer than last time, and recognizes nothing. "The model is generating an answer" is a helpful illusion — the reality is a handful of independent evaluations of a pure function, plus an append. (In production the prefix's attention keys and values are cached rather than recomputed — the KV cache — but that is an optimization with the same semantics: the whole transcript is the input → [HF transformers cache_utils.py](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/cache_utils.py).)

Now the tally, in round numbers, so the scale is felt. Each forward pass through GPT-2 small costs about 1.2×10⁸ multiply-adds per token position it processes — roughly 85M spread across the 12 blocks, plus 38.6M more in the tied output projection of 1.5. The first lap processes all seven prompt tokens at once; every lap after that, thanks to the KV cache, only has to process the one new token just appended. Four laps write the four-token answer: the first processes the 7 prompt tokens, the next three each process one fed-back token (' Paris', then ',', then ' France'); the final '.' is that fourth lap's _output_, never fed back for a fifth. That's 7 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 10 token positions in total. **Call it 1.2 billion multiply-adds to write " Paris, France."** The number matters because of what it excludes: no step in that billion looked anything up. No database, no document, no saved answer — only weights multiplying vectors. Yet what came out is correct.

So Act 1 ends on the only honest response to that: where did a pile of stencil weights get an opinion about where the Eiffel Tower actually is? **The answer is in where the weights come from.**

### 1.8 Beyond this model

Two features common in current models didn't make it into GPT-2 small's design, plus a note on scale.

**Mixture of experts.** 1.4 flagged a cost problem: every parameter in every block runs on every token, so a bigger model costs more per token to match. The fix is to notice that most detectors are irrelevant to most tokens — ' Tower' has no need of whatever machinery handles, say, Python syntax — so a model can _store_ far more detectors than it _runs_. A **mixture of experts (MoE)** replaces a block's single MLP with N full-size detector banks — experts — and a small learned router that picks which few run on each token. The router scores every expert per token; the top-k (top-2 is common) actually compute; the rest stay idle, untouched for this token. Pick a token below and watch the routing change, and watch most of the eight experts simply not fire:

<MoERoutingDemo />

The demo's expert labels ("leans function words", "no clear theme") are deliberately vague, because that is the honest picture: real experts are not human-legible specialists — the same superposition that smears 1.2's detectors smears expert roles too. What is _not_ vague is the arithmetic: parameters you store but don't run cost memory, not compute, so an MoE model can hold hundreds of billions of parameters of knowledge while each token pays for only a fraction of them. The hospital metaphor gets the economics right: **triage** — employ every specialist, send each patient to two.

**Rotary position embeddings.** 1.1's learned 1,024×768 position table is GPT-2's answer to "where am I in the sequence"; most current models answer it differently, rotating the query and key vectors as a function of distance instead (RoPE) — one more piece of learned arithmetic rather than a lookup table, and one that generalizes better to sequences longer than any seen in training.

**Scale**, labelled for comparison: GPT-2 small, the model in every figure on this page, has 124M parameters and a 1,024-token context. A frontier model in 2026 runs hundreds of billions to low trillions of parameters (often an MoE, so only a fraction run per token) with context windows in the hundreds of thousands of tokens. The mechanism is the same shape throughout; what changes is how many detectors, how many heads, how many blocks, and how much text they were shown.

## Act 2 — Training: where the weights come from

### 2.1 One step, felt

Every weight in every matrix of Act 1 starts as random noise, and training is the process that turns noise into the thing that knows the Eiffel Tower is in Paris. The process is one move repeated absurdly many times. Walk the move slowly, because each clause of it is a mechanism you now own: take a scrap of real text from the training set; feed the model everything up to some position (the causal mask of 1.2 guarantees it cannot peek past); get its predicted distribution for the next token (1.5); then compare that prediction against the token the text _actually_ contains.

The comparison is a single number called the **loss** — cross-entropy: the negative log of the probability the model gave to the true token. Assigned it 90%? Loss near zero. Assigned it 1%? Loss huge. So "the model is wrong" has been converted into "a number is too big", and that conversion buys the crucial move: **a number that depends on the weights can be _differentiated_ with respect to the weights.** Calculus answers, for every single weight, "if this weight rose a hair, would the loss rise or fall?" — and **backpropagation** is the algorithm that computes that answer for all of the hundreds of billions of weights in one backward sweep through the stack. The nudge, then, is no mystery: step every weight a hair _downhill_.

Feel the move yourself below. The setup is our own phrase as a training scrap: context `The Eiffel Tower is in`, true next token ` Paris`. The bars are the model's current, wrong, prediction — five candidates standing in for the full 50,257-entry vocabulary, renormalized; the loss is computed live from them; every press of the button takes one real gradient step. Watch ` London` outscore ` Paris` at the start, a plausible wrong guess rather than a random one, and flip only once enough steps have run. One simplification to keep in view, and the demo states it too: your press nudges the five output scores directly, while in the real model the nudge lands on the _weights_ upstream — the stencils of 1.2, the coordinates of 1.1 — and propagates through the whole stack to move these same bars.

<TrainingStepDemo />

Two scale-ups take you from this toy to the real thing. First, the demo trains one position; a real document trains _all_ positions at once — because the causal mask means every position's prediction is legitimately blind to its future, one 1,000-token document is 1,000 simultaneous lessons with no human labeling anything. That is the causal mask's dividend, and it is the entire reason raw internet text works as a teacher. Second, repeat over ~10¹³ tokens. The right image for the result is a radio with **millions to hundreds of billions of dials** (one per weight: 124 million for our GPT-2 small, hundreds of billions for 1.8's frontier models), every dial nudged a fraction toward less static on every scrap — no dial ever _set_ to a fact, knowledge existing only as the accumulated drift of all dials at once.

### 2.2 The data is the behavior

Watch what direction every nudge points: _toward the training text_. Each step makes the model assign slightly more probability to what real documents actually said, and nothing else ever touches the weights. Accumulate trillions of such steps and the conclusion is not optional — **the model's behavior is the shape of its training data.** It answers the way its documents answered, in the proportions they answered, with the blind spots they had.

The same arrow explains the failure mode. Where the data was thick — a million mentions that the Eiffel Tower is in Paris — the nudges were many, and the model's geometry (1.1) is pinned down tightly there. Where the data was thin — one mention of some obscure API — the nudges were few, so the model _interpolates_: it produces the smooth, plausible continuation that its neighbors in 1.1's geometry suggest — the space, stretched over a gap. Interpolated text sounds exactly as confident as pinned-down text, and that is confabulation, mechanically: not lying, just geometry stretched over a gap. This is also why "compression" is the honest name for what training did — ten trillion tokens pressed into hundreds of billions of weights loses detail by arithmetic necessity, and what remains is not the documents but a **lossy, generative index** of them.

There is one more gap between what we have built and what you actually use. A model trained on raw internet _continues_ text — that is all its objective ever asked. Our phrase was already a document fragment, so ' Paris' was the right continuation. Now hand it "Is the Eiffel Tower open right now?", the question 4.1 will send. It may well continue with "Is the Louvre open on Mondays?", because FAQ pages are common documents and a list of questions is a perfectly probable continuation. **Turning a continuer into an answerer takes two more rounds of the same nudging — only the data changes.**

### 2.3 Three stages, same dials

The figure below shows the full pipeline — one model, same weights throughout, three successive datasets, each stage nudging what the previous one built:

<TrainingStagesFigure />

Stage one you know: **pretraining** on the raw internet teaches the world and every style of saying things about it, and produces the continuer just described. Stage two fixes the continuer's habit: **supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** continues training on ~10⁵ dialogues written by hired humans in one fixed format — user asks, assistant answers — until "continue the document" and "answer the user" become the same act. Stage three changes something deeper. In **reinforcement learning (RLHF, or RLAIF with a model as the rater)**, the unit of feedback is no longer the next token — **whole answers get scored**, by human preference or a learned model of it, and the nudges now favor _answers people prefer_ over _text that is merely probable_. Imitation trains what text looks like; RL trains what an answer should have been. Education, apprenticeship, performance review.

That last stage is why an assistant feels like _someone_. Preference-tuning carves a durable groove: among all the voices the pretraining data contains, certain helpful, consistent personas become the easy ones for the model to produce. File the word **character** — Act 4 shows you where the character actually lives.

**So training explains where the knowledge sits: in the data, pressed into dials. What it does not yet explain is competence — why a next-word guesser can write a Python function that runs. That takes one more idea, and it is the best one on this page.**

## Act 3 — Emergence: why prediction became competence

### 3.1 The winning strategy is understanding

Here is the idea, standing alone: **when the only way to keep predicting text is to model the thing that produced the text, the optimizer builds a model of the producer.** Prediction is the objective; modeling the world behind the text is the winning strategy the objective forces.

See it in a concrete continuation problem. The training set contains millions of files like this:

```python
prices = [4, 7]
total = sum(prices)
print(total)   # prints
```

Predict the next token. Syntax is no help — after `# prints `, any number is grammatical. Statistics of nearby words is barely help. The only strategy that gets this _reliably_ right, across millions of such files with different numbers, is to track what the code does: hold the list, perform the sum, know the answer is `11`. So gradient descent, which does not care about understanding and only cares about loss, keeps whatever internal circuitry acts like a tiny interpreter — because that circuitry is what lowers the loss. The same argument runs for chemistry papers (predicting them rewards machinery that respects valence), legal text, chess notation. Whatever regularity generated the text, loss selects for internal machinery that mirrors it.

This is why "it's just predicting the next word" is simultaneously true and empty: it names the objective while missing what the objective _selects for_. And it dissolves the mystery that closed Act 1 — the billion operations contained the fact that the tower is in Paris, and the grammar to say it, because predicting text about a landmark is only possible with facts about it compressed inside.

The claim "circuitry that acts like an interpreter" should trigger your no-magic alarm, so place it on the ladder you have already climbed:

<LadderDiagram />

You have personally visited every rung: floats → matrix multiplies → an attention head, Q·K into weights into V (1.2) → one block, attention plus MLP (1.2, 1.3) → a stack of blocks (1.4) → logits and the distribution (1.5). The new claim slots _inside_ the middle rungs: **circuits** — structures wired out of heads and detectors, selected by loss, implementing things like "evaluate the sum". It is the same ladder software always is — transistor → gate → ALU → instruction → C → API — different claims at each height, no rung magic, and the top of it is exactly what the prologue promised: correct Python out of matrix multiplies.

### 3.2 The skill nobody trained: learning inside the window

One emergent circuit deserves its own section, because all of Act 4 stands on it. A trained model can pick up a _brand-new_ pattern from a few examples sitting in its input and apply it — with no weight changing at all. Look:

```text
tulip → 7
cloud → 3
tulip →
```

The pairing is arbitrary — no training text binds _tulip_ to 7 — so the completion `7` cannot come out of the weights; it can only come out of the two lines above, extracted inside a single forward pass. This is **in-context learning**, and the mechanism is machinery you already own: the pattern-copying attention heads of 1.2 emit, at the current position, a query that matches the _keys_ of analogous earlier positions — "I'm completing `tulip →`; where did _tulip_ stand before, and what followed it?" — and pull forward what followed. Why did such heads emerge? Because documents are internally repetitive, so circuits that exploit a document's own earlier structure lower prediction loss on every repetitive document in the corpus — and loss keeps what lowers loss (3.1). Few-shot prompting is nothing but this circuit, used on purpose.

So weights are not the only place the model's behavior comes from: **the window programs it too, live.** **And that fact, pushed one step further, is the bridge this page promised — from the matmul all the way to a markdown header.**

### 3.3 Steering with markdown, explained from the bottom

Assemble what the previous sections built. The stack's detectors compose patterns of patterns (1.2, 1.4); among the patterns worth detecting, the training corpus made one family unavoidable: _what kind of document am I in?_ — because knowing whether this is a code review or a casual chat is one of the strongest single predictors of the next token, and whatever predicts, loss selects (3.1). So somewhere in those refined vectors, the model tracks the document's world — and the distribution at the top (1.5) is conditioned on it.

Now flip that from description to control. The model's only input is the window; therefore the window is where the "what document is this" evidence comes from; therefore **the text you put in the window is not decoration around your question — it is the address of a region of the training distribution.**

The demo below makes the address visible, and it can finally use our own running phrase throughout: three different worlds continue "The Eiffel Tower is in" three different ways. Press each of the three framings; the continuation point stays fixed; only the framing above it changes (the five bars stand in for the full vocabulary, renormalized). Watch the top of the distribution flip from ' Paris' (52%) under a travel guide's `## Getting around Paris`, to ' Las' (46%) under a Las Vegas hotel guide's own letterhead, describing the hotel's own tower replica rather than the real one, to ' my' (44%) under a Minecraft build log, where "my world" is the natural continuation. The numbers are illustrative rather than measured, as the demo says — but the effect is real and you can reproduce it with any base model.

<WorldPickerDemo />

That is the entire mechanics of "prompt engineering", with no mystique left. A travel guide's header, a hotel's letterhead, a build log's timestamp — each is strong evidence about which world's documents come next, so every subsequent distribution shifts toward how that world's documents continue. A system prompt saying _you are a careful senior engineer_ steers the same way — documents written by careful engineers continue differently. Nothing was configured and no mode was switched; evidence was placed. **Steering an LLM is choosing which learned world the function continues — and every token in the window, yours or the model's own, votes on that choice.**

## Act 4 — The model in the real world

### 4.1 The chat is a file, not a session

Everything from here is <Chip variant='harness'>harness</Chip> — ordinary code arranged to exploit Acts 1–3. Start with the illusion you use daily: "a chat with the assistant." A chat _appears_ to be a session with a stateful someone; Act 1 proved the model is a stateless function; so the statefulness must be manufactured, and it is — by a file.

Here is one turn as the harness actually assembles it. Before your first word, the harness writes a **system prompt** — a standing instruction, authored by the product's developers, that opens the file and anchors the document-world (3.3: a deliberately placed address, and it arrives first). Then your message, then the model's reply — which begins with a **THINKING** block: tokens the model generated to work things out before answering. They are ordinary tokens in the file (that is all they are); the chat UI merely hides them from you:

<ChatTranscript
  messages={[
    { role: 'system', content: 'You are a helpful assistant.' },
    { role: 'user', content: 'hello' },
    {
      role: 'assistant',
      content: 'A casual greeting — answer warmly and briefly.',
      thinking: true,
    },
    { role: 'assistant', content: 'Hi! How can I help?' },
  ]}
/>

Now you send a second message. The model remembers nothing of the first turn — statelessness, 1.7 — so the harness has exactly one option: re-send _everything_. It re-serializes the entire file — system prompt, your greeting, the model's thinking, its reply, all dimmed below because they are re-sent verbatim — and appends your new question at the end. The whole thing goes through `f` again, from scratch:

<ChatTranscript
  messages={[
    { role: 'system', content: 'You are a helpful assistant.', dim: true },
    { role: 'user', content: 'hello', dim: true },
    {
      role: 'assistant',
      content: 'A casual greeting — answer warmly and briefly.',
      thinking: true,
      dim: true,
    },
    { role: 'assistant', content: 'Hi! How can I help?', dim: true },
    { role: 'user', content: 'Is the Eiffel Tower open right now?' },
  ]}
/>

Tap "show what the model actually sees" on either transcript: the message blocks collapse into one flat string with role markers — because that is what the "chat" is. A file. (Whether old thinking blocks ride along in re-sends is a harness choice — many strip them to save room, a first taste of the economics in 4.3.)

Two consequences fall out immediately. First, memory demystified: **"it remembers what I said" means your words are still in the file; "it forgot" means they fell out.** Second, the character demystified (2.3's word, cashed in): the assistant is not _in_ the model — the assistant is the character this document implies, summoned by the system prompt and sustained by the transcript, and `f` merely continues documents shaped like it.

> Real chat templates are exactly this string-splicing, markers and all: [a production chat_template](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct/blob/main/tokenizer_config.json) · [HF templating docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/chat_templating).

### 4.2 Tools: the model writes, the harness acts

The essence in one sentence: **a tool call is text the model wrote, which the harness recognizes by its shape and acts on.** The model never executes anything — it cannot; it only emits tokens (1.5 is its entire output channel). So the mechanism has to be: (1) the harness puts tool descriptions into the context — more address-tokens, 3.3; (2) the model, continuing the document, writes text shaped like a function call; (3) the harness spots the shape, pauses the 1.7 loop, and runs real code; (4) the result comes back into the context as ordinary tokens and the loop resumes. To the model, a tool result is indistinguishable from user text: more input.

The diagram below shows that round trip between the four parties — note that every violet arrow is plain code:

<SequenceDiagram />

And here you can drive the mechanism yourself, one step at a time, on our own running phrase: ask whether the Eiffel Tower is open right now. A bare model has no way to know today's hours — live facts are exactly where recall fails most visibly — so a chat-tuned model reaches for a tool instead of guessing. The model's lines in this walkthrough are scripted, but the two harness moves are performed for real in your browser: the parser that recognizes the call's shape is a live regex, and the tool itself is an honest stub — it ignores its argument and always returns the same fixed hours, not a real opening-hours API. Watch step 4 catch the shape and step 5 hand back that fixed answer.

<ToolCallDemo />

Now connect this to confabulation (2.2), because tools are its engineering fix. A bare model _recalls_ — interpolates its lossy index, smoothly and sometimes wrongly. A tool result pasted into the window lets it _read_ instead, and 3.3 explains why reading wins: tokens in the window are evidence that steers the distribution, so verified tokens steer it toward the truth. The same trick under the name **retrieval (RAG)** does it for documents: search first, paste the findings into the window, let the model answer from what is in front of it. You cannot patch the weights per-question — you can always patch the window.

### 4.3 Agents: the same loop, iterated

An agent is not a new kind of model. **An agent is the 1.7 loop plus the 4.2 shape-detection, run until the task is done:** emit → act → observe → emit. What makes the observations _usable_ is 3.2 — in-context learning is the circuit that lets lap N+1 exploit the pattern of what lap N's tool returned. The figure below animates the cycle; the bar in the middle is the constraint the whole trade lives under:

<AgentLoopFigure />

That bar is the **context window**, and it is finite for concrete reasons: attention compares positions pairwise, so cost grows steeply with length, and training only ever taught the model sequences up to a fixed size. Finite window plus 1.7's append-only loop equals a budget: every lap deposits tokens — tool calls and especially tool results are big — and the bar fills. When it nears the top, the harness must **compact**: drop or summarize old content to make room. Whatever it drops is gone from the model's world — not stored anywhere else, because on every lap the model _is_ only the file it is shown (4.1).

Note that long conversations degrade in two different ways, and only one is about room. The first is overflow: content fell out of the window, so it does not exist. The second is steering (3.3 cutting the other way): every token in the window votes on the document-world, including your dead ends, the tool's error dumps, and the model's own earlier mistakes — a window full of junk addresses a junk region of the distribution. Running out of room loses information; keeping the wrong things poisons the rest. Good harnesses manage both, and much of the difference between agent products lives in that management rather than in the model.

**Read the TLDR's three gifts once more, now that each has a mechanism: memory is the context window (4.1) — a file, re-sent. Hands are tools (4.2) — text, recognized by shape. The steering wheel is everything you put in the window (3.3) — evidence, addressing a world.**

## Epilogue — the metaphors, audited

The four metaphors that carry the page's biggest claims, called to account: what each earned, and where it would lie to you if pushed. (The smaller ones — the SKU, stencils, conveyor, triage, the dials — stay within the sections that own them.)

| Metaphor                          | Where it's exact                                                                                              | Where it lies if pushed                                                                                     | The repair                                                                                        |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| search engine                     | at the attention level it nearly is: query, keys, match scores, a blended result (1.2)                        | the _model_ retrieves nothing — no documents inside, answers are synthesized, confidence ≠ provenance (2.2) | a lossy **generative index**; when you need real retrieval, paste documents into the window (4.2) |
| "just predicting the next word"   | it is the objective, exactly (2.1)                                                                            | it names the objective and misses what the objective selects for                                            | prediction selects for modeling the text's generator (3.1)                                        |
| lossy compression of the internet | ten trillion tokens pressed into hundreds of billions of weights; confabulation where the data was thin (2.2) | decompression is passive; the model composes and answers what no document contains (3.1, 3.2)               | a compressor that had to _learn the world_ to compress its descriptions                           |
| "it feels like _someone_"         | the character is consistent, and RL made certain characters easy to summon (2.3)                              | no session, no memory between calls — statelessness is total (1.7)                                          | a character the transcript implies; the harness maintains the transcript (4.1)                    |

And so the TLDR, earned clause by clause: an LLM is a stateless text-continuation function (Act 1), trained into a lossy generative index of human text (Act 2) that had to model the world to keep predicting descriptions of it (Act 3), wrapped in a loop of ordinary code that gives it memory, hands, and a steering wheel (Act 4).

Prediction was never the point. It was the training signal and the I/O channel — both ends of the pipe. The thing that learned to predict is the thing you are steering.
